Statement of Research Interest

I'm a final-year undergraduate at IIT Roorkee, and most of the last year of my life has been spent going back and forth between two very different problems: getting a language model to correctly read 400-year-old Sanskrit manuscripts, and getting one to have a natural conversation with a nervous college student practicing for a job interview. Both of these turned out to be harder, and more interesting, than I expected, and both are why I want to be in the room at this symposium.

The manuscript work started as a research internship at IIT Roorkee under a project trying to digitize a large archive of Sanskrit texts. The OCR output on these manuscripts was rough — degraded scans, unusual ligatures, inconsistent scribal conventions — and raw transcription accuracy sat around a character error rate of 23%, which is unusable for anything downstream like search or translation. I ended up building a post-correction pipeline that combined TrOCR with a fine-tuned LLaMA-3.1-8B, using LoRA and 4-bit QLoRA so the whole thing could be trained on a single A100 without needing a cluster. That got the CER down to 14% and pushed BLEU-4 from 18.3 to 25.6 — about a 40% jump — at roughly a third of the training cost of full fine-tuning. The part I found most useful, though, wasn't the final number but the process of getting there: I benchmarked six different correction strategies side by side, from simple rule-based fixes and n-gram language models up through prompt-based LLMs, RAG, and hybrid approaches, and built the LangChain/LangGraph pipeline and FastAPI serving layer so these comparisons were actually reproducible instead of one-off scripts. It taught me, very concretely, that "using an LLM" is not a strategy — the interesting research questions are about where in the pipeline the model helps, where it hallucinates, and how you design the surrounding architecture so errors don't compound.

That question — where does a language model actually help, and where does it need scaffolding — has followed me into everything else I've built since. I'm currently building an AI communication coach for college students, which started from a fairly ordinary observation: students get almost no low-stakes practice for interviews, presentations, or difficult conversations before the moment they actually need to have them, and the feedback they do get is usually vague ("be more confident") rather than actionable. I designed it around a practice-analyze-improve loop — a student runs through a mock scenario, the system analyzes specific things like filler words, pacing, and structure rather than giving a generic score, and then the student gets a concrete next thing to try. I deliberately kept the first version simple — Next.js, FastAPI, Supabase, and the OpenAI API — because I wanted to validate with a handful of real students before building anything more elaborate, and that discipline of talking to actual users before scaling up has shaped how I think about every project since.

It's also shaped the two other things I've built this year. Startupscout is a search engine over 7,000+ VC-backed startups, and the interesting technical problem there wasn't retrieval in the abstract — it was that raw paragraph embeddings kept diluting precision with keyword noise, so I restructured the pipeline to have an LLM extract structured fields (problem, solution, industry, customer) before embedding, combined with BM25 and Cohere reranking. It picked up over a thousand organic users in its first month with no marketing, which told me the underlying problem (founders wasting days manually comparing competitors) was real. Separately, I built a five-agent LangGraph research platform — Collector, Analyst, Critic, RAG, and Synthesizer — with fault-tolerant retry loops and a dual-layer RAG setup over ChromaDB and Supabase pgvector, and exposed it through MCP so it could be driven directly from Claude Desktop. That project in particular is what pulled me toward wanting to understand multi-agent systems and orchestration at a deeper, more principled level than I currently do.

What draws me to this symposium specifically is that I've largely been learning these things by building and debugging alone or with a small group, and I'd like to hear how people actually doing frontier research think about the failure modes I keep running into — when agentic pipelines should be trusted versus constrained, how RAG systems should be evaluated beyond accuracy on a benchmark, and what it takes to move from a working prototype to something robust. I'd also value simply being around other people in India working seriously on AI, since most of my collaborators so far have been remote or online. A few days of lectures, discussions, and hands-on sessions with people who've thought much harder about these problems than I have feels like exactly the kind of input I need at this stage.
